If you play games on PC, you almost certainly use Steam. And if you make games for PC, you almost certainly sell them on Steam. It's the default. It's where the players are, it's where the wishlists are, and for most developers it's the only store that really matters.

But being the only store that matters gives you a lot of power, and I want to talk about how Steam wields that power, specifically when it comes to small studios and indie developers like me.

I'll be upfront, I don't actually have a game on Steam myself, yet. But it's very much the longer term plan, both for Blood in the Sand and a few other projects I'm working on right now. So this isn't just me moaning from the sidelines, it's something that's going to affect me directly.

Because when you start comparing Steam to other platforms, a pretty uncomfortable picture starts to form. Steam actually isn't that fair to the little guy.

## The 30% cut

![Close-up of a squat steel toll kiosk with a wide slot in its front and a hydraulic claw arm on top, lit from inside by a hard white strip light, its claw…](./1.jpg)

Every time somebody buys your game on Steam, Valve takes a baseline 30% cut. Sell your game for $10, and roughly $3 of it goes straight to Valve. Whatever's left has to cover everything else: the years you spent making it, your marketing, your engine fees, and, you know, keeping the lights on.

On its own, 30% doesn't sound outrageous. It's been the "industry standard" for a long time, and PlayStation, Xbox and Nintendo all take around the same.

But "industry standard" really just means that's what everyone could get away with charging. Over the last few years, a lot of the rest of the industry has quietly moved on from it. Steam hasn't.

## Even Apple are nicer

![Medium shot from behind of a lean man in his thirties with short cropped hair, a worn hooded synthetic jacket and a small glowing implant at his left temple,…](./2.jpg)

I'm not exactly Apple and Google's biggest fan. But credit where it's due, both of them have done something for small developers that Steam never has.

Back in 2020, Apple launched the App Store Small Business Program. If you made under $1 million in the previous year, Apple's cut drops from 30% to 15%. That's half.

Google went a step further in 2021 and cut its fee to 15% on the first $1 million that every developer earns each year, no matter how big they are. Google's own announcement said that meant 99% of developers selling on Play saw their fees cut in half.

As someone who ships mobile games, that's the world I live in. And when I eventually bring Blood in the Sand over to Steam, I'll be handing over double the cut for the exact same game.

Think about that for a second. Apple, the company famous for its walled garden, is a cheaper place for an indie developer to sell than Steam.

Steam has nothing like this. There's no small creators program, and no reduced rate on your first million. Whether you're a solo developer selling a few hundred copies or a massive studio selling millions, you start at exactly the same 30%. Talk about a kick in the teeth.

## Look outside Steam

![Medium shot of a narrow open-fronted market stall of corrugated metal lit by a single hanging bulb, a young woman with a cybernetic arm leaning on its counter…](./3.jpg)

It looks even worse when you step outside of games entirely. Take Bandcamp, the store a lot of independent musicians use to sell their music directly to fans. Bandcamp takes 15% on digital sales, and once you've made $5,000, that drops to 10%. On Bandcamp Fridays they waive their cut completely.

So a musician selling their very first album pays half of what Steam charges. And once they've made five grand, they're only paying 10%, a third of what Steam takes.

And you don't even have to leave PC gaming to find a better deal. The Epic Games Store takes 12% as standard, and since June 2025 it takes nothing on the first $1 million each game makes per year. Zero. If you're a small indie developer on Epic, you keep all of it.

And Epic isn't alone. itch.io, the go-to home for indie games and game jams, goes one better and lets you choose how much of each sale it takes. The default is 10%, and if you want, you can set it all the way down to zero.

To be fair, not every store has moved on. GOG, the store known for DRM-free games, does let indie developers submit their games. Its standard cut is the same 30% as Steam, but GOG will negotiate terms with developers on a case by case basis, which is more than Steam does.

The catch is that GOG is curated. They review every submission and only accept the games they think fit their store. So for most indie developers you can't just turn up and start selling, let alone negotiate.

## A discount for the giants

![Wide shot of a colossal windowless steel obelisk tower, smooth and featureless, crowned by a single glowing ring of light, a broad beam of light pouring from…](./4.jpg)

To be fair to Steam, it does reduce its cut. Back in 2018, Valve introduced revenue tiers. Once a game makes $10 million, Steam's cut drops to 25%, and once it passes $50 million, it drops to 20%.

Sounds great, until you look at who actually benefits. According to Alinea Analytics, around 20,000 games were released on Steam in 2025, and only about 300 of them made more than $1 million. And that's a million in gross revenue, so before Valve has even taken its cut. Not ten million. One.

The number of games that ever reach that $10 million mark is tiny, and they're almost all made by studios that are already massive. Meanwhile, the Steam Revenue Report estimates that the median paid game released on Steam this year has made just $1,728 so far.

So the discount Steam does offer goes to the developers who need it the least. It takes the biggest share from the people with the smallest margins, and gives a break to the companies that are already winning. When the tiers were first announced, plenty of indie developers called it a slap in the face, and honestly, it's hard to disagree.

## Just sell elsewhere

![Medium shot of a narrow open-fronted market stall of corrugated metal lit by a single hanging bulb, standing alone at the end of an empty alley lined with…](./5.jpg)

Whenever this comes up, someone always says "Well if you don't like it, just sell your game somewhere else. Put it on itch.io." And technically, yes, you can.

But that's like saying you don't need the Google Play Store because you can technically download and install apps on Android from anywhere you like. You can, but almost nobody does. The moment you step outside the place everyone already shops, your audience shrinks to a tiny fraction of what it was.

That's what a natural monopoly looks like. Steam has been estimated to handle around three quarters of all PC game sales. The players are on Steam, their friends are on Steam, their entire libraries are on Steam. So that's where you have to be, on whatever terms Steam decides.

It's trench warfare. Players are so dug in on Steam that getting them to climb out and try another store is a massive ask. And Epic has thrown everything at it: free games every week for players, and 0% fees on your first million for developers.

But even if you take that deal as a developer, your players still have to leave the trench they've been sat in for years, install another launcher, make another account, and start a brand new library from scratch. Most of them simply won't. So sure, you'll save on fees, but you'll sell a lot fewer copies.

And it isn't just developers grumbling about it. Valve is currently being sued in both the US and the UK over this exact 30% cut.

In the US, a class action started by indie studio Wolfire Games argues that Valve uses its dominance to keep fees high and stop other stores competing on price. And back in March, a judge cleared it to go to a jury trial.

Over here in the UK, a claim on behalf of up to 14 million gamers argues the commission is excessive, and that we all end up paying for it through higher prices. The tribunal's own judgment puts the damages, provisionally, at up to £656 million.

## In Steam's defence

![Wide shot of a colossal windowless steel obelisk tower, smooth and featureless, crowned by a single glowing ring of light, glowing warmly at dusk with a low…](./6.jpg)

I don't want this to be completely one sided, because there are some genuinely good arguments for Steam. First, it earned its position. Steam didn't become the biggest PC store by accident. It got there because for twenty-odd years it's been a really good product.

Whether it's a monopoly or not doesn't matter, the argument goes, because anyone is free to build a competitor. And people have tried. The problem is they've mostly semi-failed.

Epic undercut Steam by 18 percentage points, gave away free games every single week and paid huge sums for exclusives. And by its own admission the store still wasn't profitable five years after launching.

GOG has carved out a lovely niche, but that's exactly what it is, a niche. Which, if anything, kind of proves my point about it being a natural monopoly.

Second, Steam is much more than a sales page. You get reviews, wishlists, multiplayer and matchmaking tools, cloud saves, achievements, the Workshop for mods, community hubs and massive seasonal sales.

And you get a store that will actually push your game to players if it starts doing well, which is something the App Store has never really managed. Try building all of that yourself and see how far 30% goes.

And third, it's the cost of doing business. Steam charges 30% because it can. Developers keep paying it, players keep buying there, and Steam keeps breaking records. Alinea estimates around $11.1 billion worth of games were sold on Steam in just the first half of 2026. From a business point of view, why would you charge less?

That one's fair, Valve is a business at the end of the day. But it's also exactly why I think the small developer argument matters.

According to the Steam Revenue Report, the top 5% of games make up nearly 97% of all the revenue on Steam. That's the report's own calculation, not Valve's figures, but it also reckons about 60% of paid games have made less than $10,000 since Steam started.

So a small developer program, say 15% on your first million like Apple and Google, would barely make a dent in Valve's income. The money isn't coming from us. But for the thousands of tiny studios trying to get their first game to pay for itself, it would be huge.

## So is Steam fair to small developers?

![Medium shot of a lean man in his thirties with short cropped hair, a worn hooded synthetic jacket and a small glowing implant at his left temple, turning…](./7.jpg)

Steam is a fantastic platform, and I don't think anyone seriously wants it to disappear. But it charges the smallest developers the biggest cut, saves its discounts for studios making tens of millions, and sits on a market where "just go somewhere else" isn't a real option.

Apple, Google, Epic and even Bandcamp have all found a way to give the little guy a break. Steam hasn't, and right now, it doesn't have to.
